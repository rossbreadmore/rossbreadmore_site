Most organisations say they want better storytelling. What they usually mean is better slides.

I've sat in enough all-hands presentations, design reviews, and executive briefings to know what bad storytelling looks like in the wild. It's not a lack of effort — the decks are usually immaculate. It's something else. A fundamental confusion between *reporting* and *narrative*. Between telling people what happened and giving them a reason to care.

## The mistake almost everyone makes

The default structure for a work presentation is chronological. Here's the problem. Here's what we did. Here's what we found. Here's what we recommend. It's logical. It's thorough. And it almost never lands.

The reason is simple: chronology is the structure of a diary, not a story. A story requires stakes. It requires a moment where something could go one way or another. It requires you to be honest about what you didn't know, what you got wrong, what surprised you. The clean linear narrative — problem identified, solution designed, outcome delivered — erases all of that. And in erasing it, it also erases the thing that makes people actually engage.

I've watched brilliant work die in rooms because the person presenting it couldn't bring themselves to say: *we were genuinely uncertain about this*. That admission of uncertainty is not weakness. It's the thing that makes the resolution meaningful.

## What institutions are afraid of

There's a reason corporate storytelling defaults to the safe version. Organisations are deeply uncomfortable with ambiguity on display. Saying "we didn't know" feels like saying "we weren't in control." Showing tension feels like showing failure. So everything gets smoothed out before it reaches the room.

The irony is that the smoothing achieves the opposite of its intention. A presentation with no rough edges, no acknowledgement of doubt, no genuine conflict to resolve — that presentation feels untrustworthy. The audience knows the work wasn't frictionless. When the presenter pretends otherwise, they stop listening.

The most effective communicators I've worked with — the people who could actually move a room — all had one thing in common. They were willing to name the thing that was genuinely hard. Not to dwell on it, not to catastrophise it, but to bring it into the room and let the audience sit with it for a moment before showing the way through.

## What I learned from making films

I make videos. This is, by some distance, the thing that surprises people most when they find out. Executive director at JPMorgan by day, filming things on a GoPro at the weekend.

But the two things are not as separate as they sound. Filmmaking is an education in attention — in what an audience actually watches versus what you intend them to watch. You learn quickly that the frame contains more than the thing you're pointing at. That pace matters as much as content. That the moment of silence before a reveal does more work than the reveal itself.

The most useful thing filmmaking taught me about communicating at work is this: the audience's emotional state is your responsibility, not theirs. If they're confused, you lost them. If they're bored, you lost them. If they're not sure why any of this matters, you lost them — and no amount of detailed methodology is going to win them back.

You have to earn attention before you spend it.

## The one practical thing

I've given this a lot of thought, and I keep coming back to the same answer: before you write a single slide, write one sentence that answers the question *why should anyone care about this?*

Not "what is this about." Not "what did we do." Why should anyone in that room care. If you can't answer that in one sentence, you're not ready to present. The work might be done, but the story isn't.

Once you have that sentence, everything else organises itself around it. The detail that supports it stays. The detail that doesn't can go in the appendix. The structure reveals itself. The room follows.

It sounds obvious. Almost nobody does it.
