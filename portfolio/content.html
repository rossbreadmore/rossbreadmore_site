"Doing the hard work to make it simple" is about as close as I've got to a personal mission statement, and it plays out at both ends of the job. I can run a room through a genuinely difficult, ambiguous problem and get it into a plan — then stay close enough to the engineers and product people building it to keep the work honest all the way to launch. I'm just as comfortable standing in front of a leadership team making the case in three sentences, pushing back where a plan doesn't hold up, as I am heads-down in the detail myself.

  • Facilitation through to delivery: I get a room of conflicting views into a plan, then stay hands-on with product and engineering until it ships.
  • Executive storytelling: turning strategy into a story a room will act on, from a TEDx stage to a board update.
  • Hands-on craft: trained as an illustrator. Still happiest sketching a flow or writing the copy myself.
  • Positive challenge: I'll push back on a leadership team, a roadmap, or my own team — without losing the room.
  • Team building: built design teams from scratch three times over. The people I hire go on to lead elsewhere.

    Case study: one surveillance service from two vendor platforms

    The problem

    Communications surveillance ran on two vendor platforms, one for voice and one for e-communications, propped up by manual exports. Most of their wider features went unused, hand-offs between colleague teams were slow and lossy, and translation options were limited.

    What I designed

    I personally designed the overall information architecture of the MVP, and the layout and flow of every key screen. The two artefacts that carried the work were a service design blueprint and an interactive prototype.

    Around that, I set up a dedicated UX team and brought technology leadership together on a clear architectural agenda, so platform decisions served the service vision and not the other way round.

    The hard part: common ground

    Each vendor platform had its own way of handling and labelling the same key functions. I took the two apart function by function, compared how each handled and named things, and designed the common ground: one model and one vocabulary that works for voice and e-comms alike.

    How we worked

    • Research first: we sat with analysts and learned the work as they do it.
    • Prototypes in code: AI-assisted working prototypes let us test ideas and directions in days, not quarters.
    • Reuse over reinvention: we used AI to trial components from sister teams quickly, which shortened delivery.
    • Blueprints as the shared map: service design views kept technology, product and the business aligned on one picture.
    • AI for synthesis: I feed systems maps, architecture diagrams, code repositories and strategy papers into AI to extract insight, then use it to score possible interventions.

    AI in the service

    AI does two jobs for the analyst. It translates, in the underlying technology, and it summarises each alert inside the interface so a colleague can see quickly what they are looking at. Getting AI into a live surveillance workflow was a shared process, and we brought every type of colleague into it.

    Winning the decision

    The prototype did the persuading. Confidence in the MVP is what enabled senior sign-off on a single solution for voice and e-comms.

    What it looks like

    I can't show the real service. These screens are an illustrative concept I built for this portfolio, for a fictional firm with invented data, to show the kind of thinking involved: one queue across voice, email and chat, translation built into review, and hand-offs that carry their evidence with them.

    Results

    • Shipped: the working service went live in 2026.
    • For the business: the vendor tool was retired, with an eight-figure annual saving, and the architectural vision was realised.
    • For analysts: measured on time per review, false positives and languages covered. I can't publish the figures.
    • For the platform: trade surveillance is now adopting the patterns I set.

    What I'd do differently

    Hold the vision harder, earlier. In the first phase I let a neighbouring team's preferences shape the information architecture, and we spent time negotiating structure that should have gone on testing it with analysts. Next time I'd establish a distinct, evidence-backed vision before opening it up, and use prototypes to settle the argument.

    The problem

    Eight years of legacy technology was capping what the product could do. Colleagues filled the gaps with manual work, and the product team had no clear direction.

    What I designed

    I designed the information architecture, the content strategy, and the key screens and journeys for both the colleague and the customer service. The hardest was customer onboarding: the number of data points we had to capture was extensive.

    Around that, I grew the product function from 2 people to 10, spanning product management, design and innovation, and selected a new banking backend provider with the CEO and CTO.

    What we learned

    Paper was the real operating system, on both sides of the counter. Colleagues managed their day from paper lists and Google Docs, and customers ran their businesses from ledgers. The design task was to replace those tools with something better, not to add another system on top.

    The key decision: mirror images

    I set the direction to design the colleague and customer experiences as mirror images of one another: a common information architecture, a familiar interface, shared components and labels, and one journey seen from two sides.

    It made for a faster build. It also changed the conversation: when a customer got stuck in onboarding, the colleague was looking at the same journey and could help.

    Trust in the score

    The machine-learning scoring tool was the tricky area. We had evidence that colleagues were gaming the system. We built trust by introducing explainability into the algorithm, and I redesigned field training so colleagues understood how it worked, but not in so much detail that they could game it.

    What we shipped

    • A new technology stack on a modern banking backend.
    • A machine-learning customer scoring tool.
    • Android apps for colleagues and for customers.
    • A simplified SMS service.
    • Customer education inside the app, on a syllabus I simplified.

    Results

    • A 37% uplift in overall product take-up.
    • Helped the company achieve regulated status in Kenya.
    • Onboarding time and customer education delivered became the key service measures.

    What I'd do differently

    Prototype from day one. We slipped into waterfall habits, and some business processes that should have been redesigned were coded into the new platform as they were.
